How to calculate the welding heat input

A 316L stainless steel tube, 3 mm thick, welded with TIG. The machine shows 75 A, the welder travels at 12 cm/min. The welding procedure asks for a value nobody has written yet: the heat input.

You do not set this number on the machine. You calculate it from three measurements and one factor. This page explains the calculation of the calculator, line by line, on this exact case.

Why measure the heat input

Heat input tells you how much heat goes into the part for each centimetre of weld. It drives the cooling rate, and so what happens to the metal.

On a ferritic steel, too little heat input cools the heat-affected zone (HAZ) fast: it hardens. On an austenitic stainless steel, too much heat input widens the heated zone and increases distortion. In both cases, the value goes into the welding procedure specification, then it is held in production.

The formula

Three measurements are enough: voltage, current, travel speed. The calculator uses the formula of EN 1011-1:

E = (U × I × 60) / (v × 1000) in kJ/cm

U is the arc voltage in volts, I the welding current in amperes, v the travel speed in cm/min. The factor 60 converts minutes into seconds, the factor 1000 converts joules into kilojoules. E is the arc energy: the heat produced by the arc, before any loss.

Thermal efficiency

Not all the heat of the arc goes into the part. Some is lost by radiation, by convection, in the nozzle or in the slag. The thermal efficiency factor k corrects the arc energy:

Q = k × E

Process (ISO 4063)Factor k
141 TIG (GTAW)0.6
111 covered electrode (MMA)0.8
131 MIG, 135 MAG0.8
121 submerged arc1.0

Q is the heat input, the value that matters for metallurgy. The same electrical settings in TIG and in MAG do not heat the part in the same way.

Worked example: 316L tube, 3 mm thick, TIG

Settings proposed by the calculator for this case: I = 75 A, U = 13 V, v = 12 cm/min.

E = (13 × 75 × 60) / (12 × 1000) = 4.88 kJ/cm
Q = 0.6 × 4.88 = 2.93 kJ/cm

The calculator shows 4.88 kJ/cm of arc energy and 2.93 kJ/cm of heat input. Same electrical settings, four travel speeds:

Travel speed (cm/min)E (kJ/cm)Q (kJ/cm)

Slowing down from 12 to 8 cm/min increases the heat input by half. Travel speed is the setting the welder changes without thinking about it, and it is the one that counts most.

For comparison, a 3.2 mm covered electrode in flat position (110 A, 24.4 V, 20 cm/min) gives E = 8.05 kJ/cm and Q = 6.44 kJ/cm.

kJ/cm or kJ/mm

The standard uses kJ/mm, the workshop often speaks in kJ/cm. The calculator shows kJ/cm. The formula and the efficiency factors are the same; only the unit changes.

1 kJ/mm = 10 kJ/cm

The 2.93 kJ/cm of the worked example are 0.29 kJ/mm. Before you compare a value with a WPS or a WPQR, check the unit.

What the calculation does not cover

A calculated heat input is not a measured heat input. Here is the frame.

  • The proposed settings are starting values for each process; the custom mode of the calculator accepts the real settings of the machine.
  • With pulsed or alternating current, the real power is better measured than calculated from average values.
  • In multipass welding, each pass has its own heat input; the calculation is for one pass.
  • The cooling time t8/5 and the expected hardness are not calculated yet.

The displayed value helps to prepare a preliminary welding procedure specification (pWPS). The qualified range is confirmed by the procedure test according to EN ISO 15614-1.

Frequently asked questions

What is the formula for welding heat input?

E = (U × I × 60) / (v × 1000) in kJ/cm, with U in volts, I in amperes and v in cm/min. The heat input is Q = k × E, where k is the thermal efficiency factor of the process.

What is the difference between arc energy and heat input?

Arc energy is the heat produced by the arc. Heat input takes the losses into account with the factor k: 0.6 for TIG, 0.8 for covered electrode, MIG and MAG, 1.0 for submerged arc.

How do I convert kJ/cm into kJ/mm?

Divide by 10. 1 kJ/mm equals 10 kJ/cm.

Which setting changes the heat input the most?

The travel speed. On the 316L tube, going from 12 to 8 cm/min raises the heat input from 2.93 to 4.39 kJ/cm.
